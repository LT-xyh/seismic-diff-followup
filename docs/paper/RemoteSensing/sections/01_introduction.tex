\section{Introduction}

A useful subsurface velocity model must represent both the broad velocity distribution that governs wave propagation and the structural variations that determine the position of geological interfaces. For seismic imaging, errors in the initial velocity field can shift reflectors, degrade focusing, and compromise the interpretation of deeper structures. For waveform-based inversion, they also influence the starting point of a nonlinear optimization whose outcome depends on frequency content, acquisition coverage, and cycle skipping \citep{stolt1978migration,tarantola1984inversion,bunks1995multiscale,virieux2009overview}. Although full-waveform inversion can recover detailed velocity variations, constructing a plausible initial model remains an important and distinct part of the seismic imaging workflow. In settings where processing and interpretation have already produced several complementary geophysical products, the central problem is how to use those products jointly to recover a coherent depth-domain velocity field.

The observations available for this task differ fundamentally from the raw waveforms usually supplied to learned seismic inversion systems. RMS velocity provides a time-domain description of cumulative propagation kinematics and carries useful information about velocity scale. Post-stack migrated sections emphasize reflector configurations, while interpreted horizons express a more selective description of interface geometry. Well logs offer local depth-domain velocity control but cover only a small fraction of the lateral extent. These sources therefore differ not only in resolution and spatial coverage but also in the aspects of the target model that they most directly constrain \citep{dix1955velocities,stolt1978migration,hampson2001multiattribute}. Their roles are complementary rather than exclusive: a migrated section can contain broader velocity-dependent information, and an RMS trend can change with structure. A reconstruction method must reconcile these observations without assuming that every modality can be interpreted as an interchangeable image channel.

Learning-based methods provide a computationally attractive means of mapping observations to a velocity model once a training distribution has been established. Convolutional inverse models and encoder--decoder architectures have demonstrated direct velocity reconstruction from seismic data, with OpenFWI offering a systematic synthetic benchmark across different structural families \citep{yang2019deeplearning,wu2020inversionnet,deng2022openfwi}. Other formulations introduce physics-based training criteria, structured mappings, or latent representations \citep{jin2022upfwi,feng2024autolinear,gupta2025gfi}. These developments largely concern the estimator that follows observation encoding. When several processed constraints are supplied together, however, an additional design question arises before the choice of estimator: how should the available information be organized with respect to the different responsibilities of reconstructing a smooth background and recovering the remaining velocity variation? A more expressive full-field predictor can learn this distinction implicitly, but it does not make that allocation explicit.

Generative reconstruction introduces another degree of freedom. Diffusion and score-based models represent conditional variability through iterative sampling, and latent approaches move part of this computation to a lower-dimensional representation \citep{ho2020ddpm,song2021scorebased,rombach2022ldm}. Flow Matching instead trains a vector field along a prescribed probability path, allowing a continuous transport process to be integrated at inference \citep{lipman2023flowmatching,liu2023rectifiedflow}. For seismic velocity building, such methods can model corrections whose spatial organization is not easily captured by a single direct regression. Yet conditional generation of the entire velocity field assigns both the broad background and localized structural variation to the same transport variable. This is a modeling choice, not an unavoidable property of generative inversion. If an observation-conditioned background is already estimated deterministically, a residual generator can instead operate on the correction that remains relative to that estimate.

Multimodal representation learning offers mechanisms for aligning inputs of different types, weighting their availability, and encouraging consistency among paired or grouped observations \citep{baltrusaitis2019multimodal,tian2020cmc,saporta2024symile}. Those mechanisms answer how the observations should be represented and fused, but not necessarily what each resulting condition should ask the reconstruction model to predict. This distinction motivates a role-aware interface rather than a hard assignment of individual modalities to particular output components. A numerical or background-oriented condition can aggregate evidence useful for large-scale velocity recovery, while a structure-oriented condition can guide the correction process. Both interfaces may still draw on every available modality; the separation lies in the downstream reconstruction responsibilities.

A second issue concerns the reference used to define the correction. A residual relative to an ideal low-pass component of the target may be convenient during training, but that reference is not known when a new velocity model must be inferred. Composing a generated oracle-relative residual with a predicted background can therefore introduce a difference between the training target and the inference-time reconstruction. Defining the residual with respect to the background prediction itself avoids changing the reference between those stages. The predicted background becomes a shared quantity: it defines the correction target under supervision, enters the residual model as conditional context, and is added to the generated correction to form the final velocity estimate. This shared-reference design, which we term \emph{prediction consistency}, provides an explicit computational link between deterministic background estimation and generative transport without making assumptions about the intrinsic difficulty of residual learning.

We develop this organization as \emph{Background-Guided Residual Flow Matching} (\method). A role-aware encoder processes RMS velocity, post-stack migrated imagery, horizons, and sparse wells to form background-oriented and structure-oriented conditions. A deterministic U-Net estimates the background velocity, after which a conditional Flow-Matching network transports the prediction-relative correction with access to the same background estimate. In full-field coordinates, the resulting construction can be interpreted as transport from a stochastic base centered on the predicted background toward the target velocity model. Its methodological significance lies in how heterogeneous geophysical evidence and reconstruction responsibilities are coupled, rather than in a new mathematical family of transport equations.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it develops a role-aware multimodal conditioning interface that retains information from each available geophysical source while separating the downstream responsibilities of background estimation and residual guidance. Second, it establishes a prediction-consistent residual formulation in which a single inference-available background estimate is reused for residual supervision, conditional transport, and final composition. Third, it evaluates this organization on eight OpenFWI-derived structural subsets under a shared 33,600-record test protocol, comparing it with adapted deterministic and generative references and examining the background/structure decomposition and learned condition diagnostics. The central methodological perspective is to \emph{predict the background and transport the prediction-relative correction}.
